### Teaching contribution 1 — Bioelectricity and Living Systems

- Slug: `bioelectricity-and-living-systems`
- Date: `2026-09-21`
- Code: `BELS401`
- Level: Advanced undergraduate / introductory postgraduate
- Teaching period: Autumn 2026
- Contribution: Practical-session design and small-group facilitation
- Instructor: Dr Victor Heinrich Frankenstein (`me`)
- Prerequisites: Introductory physiology and basic quantitative methods
- Tags: Course; Bioelectricity

**Summary:** How electrical signals help living tissues coordinate their behaviour.

**Overview:** This course introduces bioelectric signalling as a problem of biological organisation. Students examine how signals are measured, how experiments are designed, and why striking observations require careful interpretation. The emphasis is on reasoning from evidence rather than achieving a suitably dramatic laboratory atmosphere.

**Learning outcomes:** Explain basic bioelectric signalling; distinguish immediate responses from sustained function; identify confounding variables; propose a controlled experiment; communicate uncertainty clearly.

**Illustrative assessment design:** Experimental-design proposal, 40%; critical reading exercise, 30%; final explanatory essay, 30%.

**Lesson 1 — Signals, gradients and organisation** (`signals-and-organisation`, weight 10)

Living systems rely on coordinated interactions across many scales. This lesson introduces electrical signals as one part of that coordination and asks how a local measurement relates to a wider biological process. A signal can be present without performing the role we first assign to it.

Discussion task: identify three possible explanations for an observed change in tissue activity. For each explanation, describe the observation that would make you reconsider it.

**Lesson 2 — Measuring recovery** (`measuring-recovery`, weight 20)

Before claiming that an intervention restores function, define the function you expect to observe. Immediate activity, sustained coordination and stability over time answer different questions. Controls and blinded assessment help prevent a preferred interpretation from becoming the only interpretation considered.

Discussion task: design an outcome measure that would distinguish a transient response from sustained recovery. Explain its limitations.

**Lesson 3 — Designing a repeatable experiment** (`repeatable-experiments`, weight 30)

Reproducibility starts before data collection. Record the experimental conditions, specify exclusions in advance, and separate exploratory questions from confirmatory claims. An observation that cannot be repeated may still be interesting, but it requires a different description from a robust finding.

Assignment: write a one-page experimental plan with a research question, controls, outcome measures and stopping criteria. “Wait for lightning” is not an adequate methods section.

- Slug: `research-ethics-after-the-breakthrough`
- Date: `2026-02-02`
- Code: `REAB502`
- Level: Postgraduate
- Teaching period: Spring 2026
- Contribution: Guest seminar series and discussion materials
- Instructor: Dr Victor Heinrich Frankenstein (`me`)
- Prerequisites: None

**Summary:** Responsibility before, during and after ambitious research.

**Overview:** What happens when an experiment succeeds in a way its creators did not anticipate? This seminar examines duty of care, responsibility for consequences and the limits of “we followed the protocol” as a complete ethical defence. Cases are fictional, but the questions are intended to feel familiar.

**Learning outcomes:** Identify affected parties; distinguish consent from ongoing care; evaluate foreseeable and unexpected consequences; propose a responsibility plan; explain when an experiment should stop.

**Illustrative assessment design:** Case analysis, 50%; duty-of-care plan, 30%; seminar reflection, 20%.

**Lesson 1 — Permission is a beginning** (`permission-and-care`, weight 10)

Approval to begin an experiment does not settle every responsibility that follows. This lesson separates initial permission from ongoing obligations and asks who can be affected without having been included in the original decision.

Discussion task: list the parties affected by a fictional regenerative intervention. Identify whose interests are easiest to overlook.

**Lesson 2 — The unexpected outcome** (`unexpected-outcomes`, weight 20)

Unexpected results test the quality of a research team's preparation. A good response includes observation, communication, support and a clear allocation of responsibility. Surprise may explain a missing answer; it does not remove the need to find one.

Discussion task: prepare a response plan for an outcome that changes the project's ethical assumptions. State which activities should pause while the plan is reviewed.

**Lesson 3 — Knowing when to stop** (`knowing-when-to-stop`, weight 30)

Stopping criteria protect both the integrity of research and those affected by it. They should be specified while the team can still think clearly, rather than improvised at the height of excitement. A decision to stop can be evidence of good judgement.

Assignment: write a duty-of-care plan that covers monitoring, communication, support and closure. Include the person responsible for the morning after.

### N3 — Why We Retired the Lightning Rod

- Slug: `why-we-retired-the-lightning-rod`
- Date: `2024-02-14`
- Tags: Reproducibility; Laboratory practice
- Summary: Controlled conditions rarely make the best theatre, but they make better methods sections.

**Body:**

There is something undeniably appealing about an experiment conducted under a storm-darkened sky. Unfortunately, appeal is not a unit of measurement.

The Stormproof Science programme began with a straightforward requirement: another laboratory should be able to understand our conditions and repeat our procedures without consulting a weather forecast.

That meant clearer records, more careful instrument checks and less tolerance for explanations that depended on the investigator remembering an especially impressive evening. It also meant treating failed replications as useful evidence.

The lightning rod has therefore been retired from active research. It remains an architectural feature and a cautionary example. Our methods sections have become less exciting. Our meetings have become considerably more informative.
